How to spot a fake discount: reference prices, countdowns and other tricks

Signs a discount is smaller than it looks, a simple way to find the real price, and how to check a sale licence in Saudi Arabia.

Updated: 9 November 20263 min readBy the Kobon team

The short version

  • A discount percentage is measured against a “was” price, and that price may have been high to begin with.
  • The best benchmark is a price you recorded yourself before the sale, or the same item at other stores.
  • Countdowns and “only 2 left” are pressure tactics, not proof of a good deal.
  • In Saudi Arabia you can check a sale licence by scanning its barcode or on the Ministry of Commerce website.

How a discount can look bigger than it is

Every discount is calculated from a reference price. Inflate that price and the percentage looks impressive while the final price is ordinary. That’s how you get “60% off” on something that sells for about the same all year. Not every big discount is fake, but the percentage alone tells you very little.

The most common tricks

  • Pre-sale price rises: the price climbs a few weeks before the sale, then “drops” back to where it was.
  • A “was” price nobody paid: a crossed-out figure that doesn’t reflect what the item actually sold for.
  • “Up to” discounts: the headline percentage covers a handful of items; the rest get far less.
  • Resetting countdowns: a timer that runs out, then starts again when you reload.
  • “Only one left”: scarcity messages that rush you past comparison.
  • Bundle deals: “buy 3, pay for 2” only helps if you need all three.
  • Fees at the end: a low price on the page, then shipping and service fees at checkout.

A simple way to find the real price

  1. Record the price before the sale. A dated screenshot is enough, and it’s the strongest benchmark you’ll have.
  2. Compare two or three stores for the exact same item and spec. Watch for different models, storage sizes or regional versions.
  3. Work out the final price after shipping, fees and VAT, not the price on the product page.
  4. Ask yourself: without the discount, would I buy this at this price?

Checking a sale licence in Saudi Arabia

Saudi Arabia’s Ministry of Commerce regulates sales and requires a licence for them. It lets shoppers verify promotions by scanning the barcode on the licence with a phone camera, showing the discount type, percentage, duration and business details. You can also check through the Ministry’s document verification service. If a store advertises a sale without a licence, or the licence details don’t match what’s on offer, take note.

Bank offers and cashback

Cashback isn’t an instant discount. It arrives later, usually with a cap and conditions, so count it as a saving only if you’re sure you’ll meet them. The same goes for points and gift cards: they’re worth something only if you’ll actually use them.

If an offer feels misleading

Keep screenshots from before and during the sale, and contact the store. In Saudi Arabia you can report violations to the Ministry of Commerce through its official channels. Either way, the best protection is not buying under pressure.

Common questions

Is a big discount always suspicious?

No. Stores clear stock or launch products with genuinely deep discounts. The point is to compare the final price with a benchmark you trust, not to reject every big number.

What if the price drops after I buy?

A few stores refund the difference; most don’t. Ask customer service, and if you’re still inside the return window with an unused item, returning and rebuying may be an option.

Are price-tracking tools useful?

They can show a price history, but make sure they track the same store, the same product and your country. The simplest tool is still a dated screenshot.
